# Borrow the labs' scoring rules, not their benchmarks

The most useful things Agere can take from Anthropic, OpenAI and Google are scoring rules, cost controls and one memory lesson, not their healthcare benchmarks or their models. Their published health scores (HealthBench, MAI-DxO, MedCalc, MedQA) measure patient chat and diagnosis puzzles. No lab publishes text-to-SQL, coding or prior-authorization accuracy for its frontier model, and on Stanford's MedHELM, administration and workflow is the weakest category for every model tested. Four lab contributions transfer directly. The first is OpenAI's confidence-target grading rule (+1 right, 0 "I don't know", −t/(1−t) wrong), which sharpens the companion report's rank-3 reliability score. The second is Anthropic's statistical evaluation practice: paired differences, clustered standard errors and power analysis. The third is Anthropic's November 2026 "components of agent memory" finding that wrong notes persist until something checks them against the source of record. Applied to saved recipes, that lesson becomes a deterministic, near-zero-cost fix for Agere's two known risks: a wrong saved recipe that repeats silently, and a definition change that silently breaks saved recipes. The fourth is prompt caching, which on illustrative arithmetic cuts a frontier SQL call from about $0.12 to about $0.04. The provider terms set hard architectural limits. Anthropic's BAA does not cover its Batch, Files, Skills, Code Execution or Web Fetch APIs, or connectors and MCP servers. OpenAI's zero-data-retention terms exclude batch, files and vector stores. True zero retention on Google Vertex requires switching off the default cache. The market evidence points the same way. Labs and hyperscalers now sell BAAs, routing and citations as standard plumbing for administrative workflows. No vendor publishes analytics accuracy. Only 44% of health systems have anywhere to validate AI, and about 80% of CFOs favour existing vendors. That makes a validation harness run inside the buyer's environment the most concrete go-to-market move. Everything below is researched only: nothing has been run on Agere data, and this report is not legal advice.

**How to read the evidence marks.** The research notes record whether each source page was opened and read, or seen only in a search snippet. Figures marked **[snippet, unverified]** come from search summaries. Figures marked **[vendor-reported]** come from the company selling the product. Many late-2026 model names and scores (Claude Opus/Sonnet/Haiku 5.5, Fable 5.x, GPT-6.x "Sol/Terra/Luna", Gemini 3.x/4) come from pricing pages or from aggregator leaderboards that label them self-reported. Where leaderboards conflict, this report says so and does not name a best model.

## Lab health benchmarks measure chat and diagnosis, not governed analytics

Each lab measures what it built. OpenAI's HealthBench grades 5,000 multi-turn conversations against **48,562 rubric criteria written by 262 physicians**, with a model as grader. At launch, o3 scored 0.599 overall and only **32% on HealthBench Hard** ([arXiv 2505.08775](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.08775)). The paper also reports that o3's worst-of-16 score sits about one-third below its mean ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.08775v1)). That run-to-run spread is why a single run tells Agere little. Microsoft's MAI-DxO reached **80% on 304 NEJM case puzzles against about 20% for generalist physicians** ([arXiv 2506.22405](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.22405)). Critics note the physicians were generalists and the costs were estimates ([iatroX](https://www.iatrox.com/blog/mai-dxo-85-vs-20-critical-analysis)) **[snippet, unverified]**. A claim that the work appeared in *Science* in October 2026 with different figures rests on one blog and is unverified. Anthropic's January 2026 Claude for Healthcare launch shows MedCalc and MedAgentBench results only as charts, and it reports no accuracy for its sample prior-authorization skill ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/healthcare-life-sciences)). Secondary figures of 92.3% on MedAgentBench are **[snippet, unverified]**, as is a "40% hallucination reduction" claim from a low-quality syndicated article. Google's open MedGemma reports MedQA and EHR question-answering gains, with MedQA figures that differ by source ([MedGemma report](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.05201v3)) **[snippet, unverified]**.

Independent leaderboards disagree with each other and with the labs. In the 2025 MedHELM paper, **Administration & Workflow scored 0.53–0.63, the lowest category for all nine models**, and models did worse on EHRSQL ([MedHELM](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.23802v2)). The Q2 2026 Pacific AI snapshot put Gemini 3.1 Pro first at 0.652 and Claude 4.6 Opus eighth of nine at 0.456. That page also attributes Meta's Muse Spark to xAI, which is likely an error ([Pacific AI](https://pacific.ai/evaluating-frontier-llms-for-healthcare-with-medhelm-40-clinical-scenarios-and-a-new-q2-2026-leaderboard/)). A later snippet of the official leaderboard puts gpt-6.1-sol and claude-opus-5.5 on top ([MedHELM leaderboard](https://leaderboard.medhelm.org/)) **[snippet, unverified]**. On HealthBench Hard, three 2026 flagships sit within two points (46.2, 45.8, 44.4) ([EvidenceMD](https://evidencemd.ai/blogs/evidencemd-benchmarks)) **[snippet, unverified]**. Late-2026 overall HealthBench figures show **Claude Haiku 5.5 at 0.616 against Opus 5.5 at 0.606** ([llm-stats](https://llm-stats.com/benchmarks/healthbench)) **[self-reported, unverified]**. These sources conflict and none is confirmed on a primary system card, so this report names no best model. The pattern that does hold is that top scores have compressed and cheaper tiers often match flagships. That supports ranks 7 and 8: test small and mid tiers first, and pin model versions with a re-run of Agere's own evaluation at each upgrade.

The evidence closest to Agere's work is thin and old. Unaided GPT-4 reached **33.9% exact match on ICD-10-CM** in 2024 ([NEJM AI](https://ai.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/AIdbp2300040)) **[snippet, unverified]**. A tool-augmented coding preprint reports large gains when models can look codes up ([arXiv 2407.12849](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.12849)) **[snippet, unverified]**. On EHRSQL, an open 32B SQL model (46.8%) matches GPT-4o (45.5%) ([OmniSQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.02240)) **[snippet, unverified]**. No published Claude EHRSQL result was found. On judges, the HealthBench grader's agreement with physicians is macro-F1 **0.709**, and MedHELM's jury ICC is **0.47 (95% CI 0.10–0.69)** on only 56 instances ([MedHELM](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.23802v2)). For Agere this means two things. Code lookup should stay deterministic and governed, not handed to the frontier model. And SQL answers should be scored by executed-result match, with model judges reserved for free text and checked against clinician labels. The companion report reached the same conclusion from the EHRSQL and coding studies. The lab evidence adds that **no lab is competing on Agere's task**, so buyers cannot rely on lab benchmarks to answer it.

## Scoring rules and statistics from the labs harden ranks 3 and 8 of the companion report

The companion report's rank 3 proposes a reliability score that penalises wrong answers (EHRSQL RS(10)) with agreement-gated abstention. Its rank 8 proposes a governed-definition ablation and schema pruning. Frontier-lab work strengthens both without adding a model.

OpenAI's September 2025 paper argues that right/wrong grading trains models to guess. It proposes telling the model to "answer only if you are >t confident", scoring **+1 correct, 0 for I-don't-know and −t/(1−t) wrong**. At t=0.5 a wrong answer costs 1 point, at t=0.75 it costs 2, and at t=0.9 it costs 9 ([arXiv 2509.04664](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.04664)). The companion blog illustrates the stakes. gpt-5-thinking-mini abstained 52% of the time with 22% accuracy and 26% errors. o4-mini abstained 1% of the time with 24% accuracy and **75% errors**: about the same accuracy, nearly three times the error rate ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/why-language-models-hallucinate/)) **[snippet, unverified]**. RS(10) is in effect t≈0.91 applied everywhere. The improvement for Agere (rank 2) is to set t per decision class. A shared-savings projection or a PMPM figure that goes to a CFO gets t=0.9. An exploratory cohort count gets a lower t. Accuracy, error and abstention are reported as three numbers, not one. The model-side counterpart is a DeepMind-authored conformal abstention method. It calibrates the agreement threshold on held-out questions to a guaranteed bound on wrong answers ([arXiv 2405.01563](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.01563)), which is the calibration step the companion item already needs. For SQL, agreement should be judged by equality of executed results. That is cheaper and stricter than semantic entropy (Oxford), whose sampling costs 5–10× ([arXiv 2406.15927](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.15927)), or Anthropic's P(True)/P(IK) self-assessment, which the authors say calibrates poorly on new tasks ([arXiv 2207.05221](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05221)).

Anthropic's November 2024 guidance supplies the statistics Agere's entry rule lacks (rank 3). It asks for a standard error on every score. It asks for errors clustered on the unit that questions share, because **"clustered standard errors on popular evals can be over three times as large as naive standard errors."** It asks for paired differences on the same questions, since frontier models' per-question scores correlate 0.3–0.7. And it asks for a power analysis to size a set that can detect, for example, a 3-point difference ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/statistical-approach-to-model-evals)). Agere's questions share population definitions and templates, so naive errors will overstate how sure any "beats the LLM alone" claim is. Every ablation in the companion rank 8 (raw schema against schema plus governed definitions against semantic-layer query) should be reported as a paired difference in penalised score and cost per question, with clustered errors.

Two other lab results support the existing ladder rather than adding to it. On Spider 2.0's 632 enterprise tasks, an o1-preview agent succeeded **21.3% of the time against 91.2% on Spider 1.0** ([arXiv 2411.07763](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.07763)). Large real schemas are where free-form LLM SQL breaks down, and that is the case for recipes and governed definitions first. Google-authored CHASE-SQL reached **73.0% on BIRD** by generating several candidates and picking one with a trained selector ([arXiv 2410.01943](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01943)). Agere's agreement gate already produces candidates. A selector is therefore a small extension (rank 14), and it should be tried with a majority vote on executed results first, before any fine-tuned selector. Anthropic's "Building effective agents" advises starting with the simplest workflow and "poka-yoke" tool arguments ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)). For Agere that means typed population/date-scope and definition-ID parameters instead of free-text column names, and keeping governed answers in a fixed workflow rather than an open-ended agent. Google's ACT work trains a model to decide when to ask a clarifying question, using an ambiguous-SQL task ([arXiv 2406.00222](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00222)). Training is not justified yet, because the abstract gives no numbers. The test-set pattern is cheap and useful (rank 13): unnecessary questions cost a little, and confident answers to ambiguous questions cost t/(1−t).

## Anthropic's memory research says wrong notes persist until checked against the source

"The components of agent memory" (Anthropic Engineering, 6 November 2026) defines memory as readable context kept across sessions, in four layers: transcripts, memory notes, linked representations such as a wiki, and context injected into every session. It adds an offline "dreaming" pass that checks notes against transcripts and reorganises them ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/components-of-agent-memory)). Agere already has each layer. Run logs and versioned evidence are the transcripts. Customer clarifications are the notes. Governed definitions and the recipe library are the representations. Active tenant definitions are the injected context. On a lab-internal database task, memory raised Opus 5.5 from **0.655 to 0.838** and Sonnet 5 from 0.306 to 0.610. That shows memory helps repeated database work. It does not show that free-form memory beats a curated recipe library, which is what Agere has. The page's more important finding is about failure. A note that wrongly placed a building on the east side of a map sent the uncorrected agent into a loop it never escaped. The dreaming-corrected run finished at action 1,935. As the authors put it, "the transcript gave Claude a ground truth to validate its memory against." The page also found that one page per entity with terms quoted verbatim captured **57 of 57 contract facts against 41 of 57 for a shared table**. It found that a rigid "record only failures" rule cut the memory gain by up to 0.234. And it found that concurrent writers to one file overwrite each other.

Those findings map directly onto Agere's two known risks. The rest of the literature handles stale memory weakly. Mem0 asks an LLM to choose ADD, UPDATE, DELETE or NOOP ([Mem0](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.19413v1)). OpenAI's Agents SDK cookbook consolidates notes with "most recent wins" precedence ([OpenAI cookbook](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/agents_sdk/context_personalization)). Zep's bi-temporal model is the best engineered: it marks old facts invalid instead of deleting them and links each fact to its source episode ([Zep](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.13956v1)). None of these re-executes a stored procedure to check it. Rank 1 combines the best parts into a design that needs no LLM. Each recipe records the definition versions it depends on, valid-time and transaction-time fields, and its source run in the shared decision context. A new definition version sets dependent recipes to "needs re-verification" by a deterministic dependency check. A nightly job re-executes them against a reference cohort and flags any result change for review. This is the analytics equivalent of dreaming, and it is cheaper because the check is execution, not reflection. It needs a four-arm evaluation with seeded definition changes and a deliberately wrong recipe. The arms are no memory, embedding retrieval of past answers, current recipe reuse, and versioned reuse with invalidation. The measures are silent-reuse rate, detection rate and false invalidations. No published benchmark tests memory of executable procedures under definition change, so Agere's result would be the evidence.

Procedural-memory research supports reuse and adds one specific idea. Agent Workflow Memory reported **+24.6% and +51.1% relative success** on two web benchmarks ([arXiv 2409.07429](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.07429)). Google's ReasoningBank, which stores lessons from failures as well as successes, reported **+8.3% on WebArena and +4.6% on SWE-Bench-Verified** over a memory-free agent, and claims to beat success-only workflow memory ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/reasoningbank-enabling-agents-to-learn-from-experience/)). These comparisons are all against no memory, never against a human-verified library. That is why rank 12, pitfall memory, comes after rank 1 and must beat current recipe reuse. Anthropic's Agent Skills pattern of loading only names and descriptions until needed ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/equipping-agents-for-the-real-world-with-agent-skills)) is a token lever for the recipe catalogue. It belongs inside the caching and pruning measurement, not as a separate item. Note that Anthropic's hosted Skills API beta is outside its BAA (see below), so only the pattern transfers, implemented locally.

Memory is also an attack surface in healthcare. MINJA planted malicious records in an EHR agent's memory through ordinary queries alone. It achieved **95.6–98.5% injection success and 57–90% attack success** on MIMIC-III and eICU, and an LLM-judge defence caught 131 of 135 attacks on one agent but **0 of 135** on another ([MINJA](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.03704)). The defence that works is structural. Memory is keyed by tenant, with authorisation enforced in storage. Only governed objects (definitions, recipes, preferences) are remembered, never free-text patient content. Nothing enters shared memory without Agere's existing verification gate. Anthropic's client-side memory tool is HIPAA- and ZDR-eligible and leaves retention with the application ([Claude docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/memory-tool)). Hosted memory vendors (Mem0, Zep) would be a new external service holding customer context, so they are parked.

## Cost features pay only if they stay inside BAA coverage

The 2026 price spread makes cost engineering worth doing. Standard list prices per million tokens on 17 November 2026 run from **$0.10 input / $0.50 output for Claude Haiku 5.5 and gpt-6-luna**, through $2–$4 / $10–$20 for mid frontier models (Sonnet 5.5, Opus 5.5, gpt-6.1-sol, Gemini 4 Carbon/Argon), to **$8–$10 / $40–$50 for Fable 5.2 and gpt-6-astra** ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing); [OpenAI pricing](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing); [Gemini pricing](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/pricing)). These names and prices are as the vendor pages listed them on that date. Gemini 4 Carbon/Argon prices double on 1 December 2026, so Gemini list prices should not anchor a cost baseline.

**Prompt caching (rank 6)** is the clearest win. Anthropic charges 1.25× base input to write a 5-minute cache and 2× for a 1-hour cache, and reads cost 0.1× base, or 0.05× on Opus and Sonnet 5.5. The minimum prefix is 512 tokens on current models. Changing tool definitions, `tool_choice`, effort or structured-output format can silently drop the cache ([Anthropic caching](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching)). On illustrative arithmetic from the notes, take a frontier SQL call with a 20k-token cached prefix, 2k fresh input and 1.5k output on Opus 5.5. It costs about **$0.042 with a cache hit against $0.118 without**, before thinking tokens and the 1.1× US-residency uplift. A 1-hour write costs about $0.16 once and pays back after two reads. Analyst traffic is sporadic, so hit rate is the deciding measure, and the 1-hour lifetime should be tested against 5 minutes. Claude API caches are isolated per workspace, which maps onto per-customer workspaces. Bedrock and Google-managed Vertex isolate only at organisation level. The simple alternative is schema pruning (companion rank 8), which CHESS reported cut tokens five-fold. The two stack, so the ablation should report each alone and together.

**Effort (rank 8)** is now the main cost dial on Anthropic models. Adaptive thinking cannot be turned off on Opus 5.5. The default is "medium" on Opus 5.5 and Haiku 5.5 but "high" on most others. Anthropic says most capability is already present at "high" and that "max" can overthink structured-output tasks ([Anthropic effort](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/effort)). A 2026 study of 17 text-to-SQL configurations found execution-feedback refinement was "the only paradigm whose benefit holds universally at consistently low cost". It also found that a mid-tier model with a richer pipeline often beats a frontier model with a lean one ([arXiv 2608.28432](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.28432)), though only the abstract was read. The trial is a low/medium/high sweep against one execution-feedback retry at low effort, with effort recorded in each answer's provenance. **Cascades (rank 7)** have strong but dated support. FrugalGPT matched GPT-4 at **up to 98% lower cost** ([arXiv 2305.05176](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05176)), and RouteLLM cut cost by **over 2×** without quality loss ([arXiv 2406.18665](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18665)). Savings depend on a calibrated "accept" signal, which items 2 and 3 provide. Vendor auto-routers hide which model answered, which breaks per-answer provenance, so they are skipped.

**Batch (rank 9)** is a flat 50% discount at Anthropic, OpenAI, Google and Bedrock ([Anthropic batch](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/batch-processing)). But Anthropic's batch page marks it "not-eligible" for zero retention (29-day retention), and the BAA page lists Batch as **not covered** ([Anthropic BAA](https://privacy.claude.com/en/articles/8114513-business-associate-agreements-baa-for-commercial-customers)). OpenAI's zero-retention terms exclude batches too ([OpenAI your data](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/your-data)). Batch therefore fits nightly evaluation and recipe re-verification on synthetic or de-identified data, and cheap overnight note reading of real PHI is out on the first-party APIs unless coverage changes. **Citations (rank 11)** return exact quoted spans. Quoted text is free in output tokens, and the feature is HIPAA-ready and ZDR-eligible ([Anthropic citations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/citations)). Anthropic reports **up to 15% better recall** than custom implementations, and one customer reports source hallucinations falling from 10% to 0% ([Anthropic](https://claude.com/blog/introducing-citations-api)) **[vendor-reported]**. Citations cannot be combined with structured outputs. The baseline is a prompted quote-and-offset JSON verified by exact string match, which Agere's note ladder can already do. **Strict structured outputs** remove parse failures, but vendors publish no error rates, and one study found format restrictions hurt reasoning ([arXiv 2408.02442](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.02442)). They stay parked until Agere's own logs show envelope retries cost something. **Token counting** is free and BAA-covered, so it is worth using as a pre-call budget guard (folded into rank 5's spend accounting). Fast and priority tiers cost 1.75–2.5× for speed Mike has deprioritised.

## Provider terms draw the line Agere's architecture must follow

The BAA request page and usage policies returned 403 and are unverified. On Google Vertex, the Google Cloud BAA covers its generative AI products. Pre-GA offerings must not touch PHI. **True zero retention requires disabling the default 24-hour implicit cache**, and Grounding with Google Search keeps logs for up to 3 days regardless ([Vertex ZDR](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/vertex-ai-zero-data-retention)). That means rank 6's caching savings are not available on a zero-retention Vertex deployment. Bedrock is HIPAA-eligible under the standard AWS BAA, and model providers cannot see prompts. But Bedrock **retains all Claude Fable 5.x traffic for up to 30 days** unless the customer qualifies for its Enterprise Frontier Safeguards terms ([Bedrock abuse detection](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/abuse-detection.html)). Azure Foundry includes a BAA in its Product Terms. Its Global and DataZone deployments widen where processing happens, and **Batch is a Global deployment type** ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-foundry/responsible-ai/openai/data-privacy)). The consistent architectural rule, which is rank 5, is that PHI goes only through synchronous, stateless, US-region inference calls, with every answer recording its route. Batch, file stores, hosted code execution, web fetch, connectors and MCP stay on synthetic or de-identified data.

Anthropic's usage policy (effective 12 November 2026) defines "High-risk AI Recommendations" in healthcare and insurance. These include triage or waitlist priority; eligibility, denial or discontinuation of care programs; and claims, coverage, prior-authorization and medical-necessity decisions. They require meaningful review by a qualified person and disclosure that AI was used. Internal research and analytics drafting, and fixed rules applied without model judgment, are excluded ([Anthropic AUP](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/aup)). Agere's aggregate population and VBC analytics look like the excluded category. Member-level outreach or care-program eligibility lists look like the high-risk category and need a human-review step and disclosure (*confirm with counsel*). The regulatory picture points the same way. Section 1557's §92.210 on patient-care decision-support tools remains in force after the October 2025 vacatur of other provisions ([Federal Register, June 2026](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-06-02/html/2026-11015.htm)). CMS requires Medicare Advantage coverage determinations to rest on the individual's circumstances ([Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en-gb/knowledge/publications/644bd9a2/cms-clarifies-medicare-advantage-organizations-use-of-ai-and-algorithms-in-coverage-decisions)) **[snippet, unverified]**. Colorado's SB26-189 makes health care a "consequential decision" area. From **1 January 2027** it requires developers to give deployers documentation of intended uses, training-data categories, limitations and human-review instructions ([Colorado General Assembly](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb26-189)). California's SB 1120 requires a licensed clinician for medical-necessity denials, and Texas requires disclosure of AI use in care ([JD Supra](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/new-year-new-ai-rules-healthcare-ai-9758831/)) **[snippet, unverified]**. The federal side has loosened. A law-firm alert reports the HTI-5 final rule removed the HTI-1 predictive-DSI source-attribute and risk-management requirements ([Akin Gump](https://www.akingump.com/en/insights/ai-law-and-regulation-tracker/onc-issues-hti-5-final-rule-removing-model-card-and-risk-management-requirements-for-predictive-decision-support-interventions)) **[snippet, unverified; check the Federal Register]**, and FDA's January 2026 CDS guidance eased treatment of single recommendations to clinicians ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/clinical-decision-support-software)). The FDA page's statement that "Executive Order 14434" has agencies use "Super Intelligence" in place of "AI" was observed but **not verified** and should not be repeated as fact. The product consequence is rank 10. Federal model-card mandates are receding, but state law (Colorado from 2027) and provider policy (Anthropic's AUP) now require the same documentation. Agere should ship it as part of the product and keep coverage, prior-authorization and denial features out of scope or human-gated.

## The market sells plumbing; correctness evidence is the open gap

All three frontier labs and Google Cloud now sell HIPAA-eligible healthcare offerings built on the same pattern: a BAA, no training on customer data, reference-code connectors, and administrative workflows such as prior authorization, appeals, claims and documentation. Anthropic's launch names Banner Health, Premier, Commure and others. Optum's July 2026 partnership puts Claude under claims, revenue-cycle and "next best action" work inside UnitedHealth's **$3B 2026–2027 AI program** ([HIT Consultant](https://hitconsultant.net/2026/07/13/optum-partners-anthropic-healthcare-ai/)). OpenAI for Healthcare (January 2026) lists Cedars-Sinai, HCA, UCSF and others without saying how deeply they use it ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/openai-launches-healthcare-product-line-signs-up-major-us-hospitals/)). Google's Gemini for Healthcare (10 November 2026, preview) offers read-only Epic and Oracle Health connectors, model choice across Google, Anthropic and OpenAI, "smart routing" between cheap and high-reasoning models, and "verifiable source citations", **with no performance metrics** ([Google Cloud](https://www.googlecloudpresscorner.com/2026-11-10-Google-Cloud-Launches-Gemini-for-Healthcare)). Tiered routing and citations are now marketed platform features, so they are table stakes, not differentiators. None of these offerings is positioned as governed population-health or VBC analytics, and none publishes analytics accuracy.

This builds on the competitive-bar report's finding that no healthcare vendor publishes Q&A accuracy. In 2026 the incumbents moved onto lab and platform stacks. Innovaccer's natural-language analytics now runs on Snowflake Cortex text-to-SQL ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260127889694/en/Innovaccer-and-Snowflake-Partner-to-Drive-Enterprise-AI-Adoption-Across-Healthcare)) **[snippet, unverified]**. Epic's strongest analytics claim is speed: **SlicerDicer SideKick cut query creation time by up to 85%** at one customer ([Healthcare IT Today](https://www.healthcareittoday.com/2026/08/20/great-stats-from-epic-ugm/)) **[vendor-reported]**. Komodo's Marmot let NCQA test a new HEDIS measure in **about one month against a historical 2–3 months** ([Komodo](https://www.komodohealth.com/?p=21815)) **[vendor-reported]**. That is the best market template for a claim: a credible measure steward, a concrete workflow and a time baseline. It still has no correctness figure. AWS made Amazon Quick HIPAA-eligible with row-level security and chat logging ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/deploy-ai-in-a-hipaa-eligible-environment-with-amazon-quick/)), which makes governance plumbing a deployment prerequisite rather than an edge. 2026 startup funding went to claims, payment-integrity, utilisation-management and risk-adjustment automation (Alaffia $55M, Anterior $40M, Keebler $16M) ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/02/03/alaffia-health-raises-55m-expand-agentic-ai-health-plan-claims-operations/)) **[snippet, unverified]**. No funded startup was found positioned as verified natural-language VBC analytics. The open gaps are published correctness and abstention evidence, governed VBC definitions shipped pre-curated, a validation harness buyers run themselves, and cross-source answers not tied to one EHR or cloud. The threat is that incumbents can add verified-query curation quickly, since Cortex already has it. The durable assets are pre-curated VBC recipes and published evidence, not the mechanism.

Buyer evidence turns these gaps into go-to-market moves. Health systems have nowhere to test. Per CCM/KLAS, **92% run pre-deployment testing but only 44% have a sandbox to validate accuracy, safety and drift**, and **14 of 27** systems cite inconsistent metric definitions across departments as a top barrier ([HIT Consultant](https://hitconsultant.net/2026/08/06/ccm-klas-research-health-system-ai-governance-testing-report/)). That is the case for rank 4. The pilot is Agere's scorecard run on the buyer's de-identified extract inside their environment, so the buyer's governance committee gets the validation platform it lacks. Incumbents have the advantage. **Nearly 80% of CFOs say an existing vendor, or a partner of one, has a significant advantage**, and 70% are more comfortable sharing data with existing partners ([HFMA](https://www.hfma.org/health-system-adoption-of-ai-outpaces-internal-governance-and-strategy/)). About 60% of providers buy EHR-first when the module is "good enough" ([Bain](https://www.bain.com/insights/healthcare-it-investment-ai-moves-from-pilot-to-production/)). That argues for running inside or alongside the buyer's existing warehouse, and for positioning as complementary to the lab offerings, since Agere's governance layer can sit above any of their models.

Payers are a slower and smaller market. They account for **5% ($50M) of 2025 healthcare AI spend**, have 14% deployment and an **11.3-month AI buying cycle against 6.6 months for health systems** ([Menlo Ventures](https://menlovc.com/perspective/2025-the-state-of-ai-in-healthcare/)) **[investor-reported]**. Yet they rank population analytics, VBC risk and quality tools among their priorities, and they compare total cost of ownership against building in-house ([Bain](https://www.bain.com/insights/healthcare-it-investment-ai-moves-from-pilot-to-production/)). An inference from these figures: lead with risk-bearing providers and ACOs, and bring a build-versus-buy cost comparison to payers. ROI framing matters because the most credible independent body is sceptical. PHTI's 2026 work concludes that administrative AI under fee-for-service speeds and inflates transactions. It links one AI scribe to a 5% rise in Level 5 visits and about $1,004 in extra monthly billing per doctor ([HIT Consultant](https://hitconsultant.net/2026/10/14/phti-report-driving-adoption-high-value-ai-healthcare-caroline-pearson-roadmap-payment-reform/)). A buyer who has read that will discount revenue-uplift claims. Agere's ROI case belongs in outcome-aligned terms: shared savings, avoided utilisation, analyst hours and measure-testing time. Because buyers lack the means to measure ROI, the measurement method should be written into the pilot contract. KLAS's VBC enablement winners "operationalize insights" with hands-on service, and the analytics of one lower-rated vendor were called "broad but insufficiently actionable" ([HIT Consultant](https://hitconsultant.net/2026/01/29/klas-report-value-based-care-enablement-winners-2026/)). Onboarding and the work-and-follow-up binding therefore count as much as accuracy. Finally, the MIT NANDA "95% of pilots fail" figure is preliminary, cross-industry and based on unreleased data ([AI Governance Library](https://www.aigl.blog/state-of-ai-in-business-2025.md)). Quoting it would undercut an evidence-first pitch. The defensible line is that most healthcare AI is still at pilot stage (27% of revenue-cycle teams deploying at scale, 53% piloting ([HFMA](https://hfma.org/technology/most-healthcare-organizations-are-adopting-ai-in-the-revenue-cycle-hfma-poll)) **[snippet, unverified]**) and that few buyers can measure its return.

## Gaps in this research

Several gaps limit these conclusions. openai.com returned 403 throughout, so OpenAI's HealthBench blog, system cards, BAA request page, usage policy and fine-tuning wind-down are unverified. No lab or independent source reports current Claude, GPT or Gemini results on EHRSQL or MIMIC SQL, and no 2025–2026 frontier coding, claims or prior-authorization benchmark was found. The late-2026 model names and scores could not be checked against primary system cards. Anthropic's Covered Models list and full feature-eligibility table were not opened, so which models carry mandatory 30-day retention is inferred. Anthropic's training position for commercial API data was confirmed only by press quotation. The HTI-5 final rule, CMS MA FAQ, California and Texas statutes, and the FDA's four non-device CDS criteria were read only through secondary sources. "Executive Order 14434" was not verified. No source says whether a vendor like Agere, as opposed to its covered-entity customers, is directly regulated under §92.210. On memory, no benchmark tests executable procedures under definition change, and the Anthropic CL-Bench figures come from a lab-internal task with no public dataset. On cost, no vendor publishes latency gains from caching, and Bedrock, Vertex and Azure Claude price tables were not extracted. On the market, HLTH 2026 (15–18 November) announcements were not captured, many vendor claims are snippet-only, no public price exists for the lab healthcare offerings, and no survey isolates spend on conversational or population-health analytics. Nothing here was run on Agere data, and the "beats an LLM alone" half of the entry rule is untested for every item.

## Conclusion

The labs' most transferable work is the work they do on themselves: how they score, how they measure uncertainty, and how they correct memory. Their healthcare products and benchmarks aim at administrative transactions and patient chat, leaving governed population analytics unaddressed. The memory finding matters most for Agere, because it reframes the two known recipe risks. They are not a reason to doubt saved recipes. They are the general problem of stored knowledge drifting from its source. Agere can close that problem more cheaply than the labs, because a SQL recipe can be re-executed and a free-text memory note cannot. Versioned recipes with automatic invalidation turn the stack's biggest silent-failure risk into a measured, auditable process, and that same process becomes evidence in a buyer's validation pilot.

The terms research changes the cost plan more than the price lists do. The cheapest features (batch, hosted files and code execution, connectors, and on Vertex the default cache) are exactly the ones that leave BAA or zero-retention coverage. Savings must therefore come from features inside the covered path: caching on synchronous calls, effort control, cheaper tiers through a calibrated ladder, and batch used only for synthetic evaluation. On the market side, labs and hyperscalers have made BAAs, routing and citations standard. What buyers still cannot get is a vendor willing to show, on their own data and in their own environment, how often a returned number is wrong.

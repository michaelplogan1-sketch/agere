# Measure first, then mend the ladder

The research supports nine concrete, testable improvements to the Agere answering ladder, and the three with the highest expected value involve no new model. First, enlarge and protect the test sets, because 19 of 28 is too small a sample to tell an improvement from luck. Second, change how a built-in method is chosen: shortlist a handful of the roughly 60 methods, allow "none of these", and add a separate confirming check. Third, harden recipe promotion, because two models returning the same rows on one copy of the warehouse is a more optimistic signal than it looks. Below those sit four medium-value changes (send fewer questions to the last-resort agent, fit a confidence cut-off per decision instead of one global 0.8, track each recipe's record after promotion, aim the batch learner at known failures) and two lower-value ones (stricter document-quote checks, a look-up of similar past questions). The research does not support training a router or fine-tuning a small model on the few hundred logged outcomes this stack has, nor same-model voting, nor trusting a model's stated confidence. Three gaps matter most: no paper measures how often two different models agree on the same wrong rows, no paper trains a router on fewer than about 4,000 examples per domain, and paid DAIR Academy course content was not read because the connector needs the product owner's sign-in. Every figure in this report is the authors' own result on their own data; none has been measured on Agere.

## How the ranking was built and how deeply each source was read

The ranking weighs three things: whether the change targets a failure this stack has already measured, how cheap the test is against the simpler thing already in place, and how well the supporting source was read. Four reading depths appear throughout. **Full text** means the paper body was read (long papers to roughly the first 100,000 characters, so appendices may be missing). **Abstract only** means headline numbers without methods or limitations. **Snippet** means a search-result summary that was never opened; treat it as a lead. **DAIR summary** means DAIR.AI's weekly one-paragraph digest of a paper whose text was not opened; this is weaker than an abstract, and several of the arXiv numbers DAIR links to have unusual sequence numbers that should be confirmed before anyone cites them ([DAIR 2026 list](https://github.com/dair-ai/ML-Papers-of-the-Week/blob/main/years/2026.md)).

| Rank | Change | What the user would notice | Test against what is in place | Best evidence and depth |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Bigger, untouched test sets; wrong answers penalised; repeat runs | Nothing directly; every later claim becomes checkable | Re-score current ladder under the new rules before changing anything | Full text (data-agent benchmark, library-drift papers); abstract (TrustSQL, EHR-ChatQA) |
| 2 | Method choice as shortlist, "none of these", then confirm | Fewer plausible-but-wrong methods answering | Held-out method questions: current single choice vs three-step | DAIR summary (Skill++, Demystifying Agent Skills); abstract (REFLEX, ToolRet) |
| 3 | Harder promotion gate: several test databases, a non-SQL second opinion | Fewer saved recipes that are quietly wrong | Re-run already promoted recipes through the harder gate; count failures | Full text (SpotIt, unanimous-jury study); abstract (DPC, conformal-oracle study) |
| 4 | Fewer questions reaching the last-resort agent | Fewer 17 to 55 second, unverified answers | Share of traffic and spend at the last rung, before and after | Abstract (RouteNLP, Crystallization, semantic-layer study); snippet (prompt caching) |
| 5 | A fitted cut-off per decision type instead of 0.8 everywhere | Fewer needless escalations and fewer wrong accepts | Held-out log slice: wrong-accept and escalation rates vs 0.8 | Full text (UCCI, Switchcraft, vCache); DAIR summary (JEV-as-a-Judge) |
| 6 | Per-recipe track record, careful retirement, stricter matching key | Recipes that go stale get caught | Replay logged questions: wrong-reuse count vs never-retire | Full text (Library Drift, Ratchet, OLAP caching) |
| 7 | Batch learner writes the query first and targets known failures | More new question shapes learned per run | New distinct shapes promoted per 100 generated questions | Full text (SENTINEL); abstract (SQL-Zero, ClinicalBench) |
| 8 | Document readers check that the quote supports the claim | Counts that exclude negated, family-history and historical mentions | Hand-labelled trap set vs the current quote-exists rule | Full text (VA validation study, Task Cascades); abstract (VERGE, ClinicalBench) |
| 9 | Look up similar past questions before climbing the ladder | Possibly faster routing; expected gain small | Time-split replay of the log vs fixed ladder order | Full text (kNN router, Routing Plateau, Switchcraft) |

## Nineteen of 28 cannot tell an improvement from luck

The first change is to the yardstick. Nineteen of 28 is 68%, and by standard arithmetic the 95% range around that figure runs from about 49% to 82%, so a routing change needs to flip roughly six questions before it is distinguishable from noise. Every test proposed below depends on a larger held-out set of method questions, and on a second set that the learning loop never touches. The regression set that gates recipe promotion is tuned by construction, since recipes are kept only when it does not get worse; the library-drift papers keep the tasks used for building entirely apart from the tasks used for scoring and run a no-library control beside every result ([Library Drift, full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.19576v1)).

Four scoring practices from the literature are cheap to adopt. Score wrong answers below abstentions, as the TrustSQL benchmark does, so that a supported "unknown" is not punished like an error ([TrustSQL, abstract only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.15879)). Run each question several times: on a hospital-records benchmark the authors report agents succeeding at least once in five tries over 90% of the time on one task type, while success on all five was up to about 60 points lower ([EHR-ChatQA, abstract only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.23415)), and an open-model study found reruns alone flip 0.2% to 3.7% of answers ([open-model text-to-SQL study, full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.29733)). Grade whole tables, not the presence of the right value: a 54-question data-agent benchmark admits its own value-containment grading does not penalise extra wrong values ([DAB, full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.20576v1)). And audit the label itself. A preregistered study found that an abstention guarantee calibrated to a nominal 10% risk on execution-based labels carried 17 to 20 points of risk once two SQL experts labelled the same items blind ([Certified Against Which Oracle, abstract only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25938)). For Agere the equivalent step is hand-checking a sample of logged answers marked successful because the query ran. What the research does not supply is a study of how much a self-improving loop inflates its own tuned regression set; that risk is inferred, not measured.

## The method menu and the promotion gate carry the most value

**Rank 2, method choice.** Today one step picks among about 60 methods and a wrong but plausible pick can be accepted. The proposed shape has three separately measurable steps: retrieve a shortlist of about five, let the typed-judgment classifier choose among them with an explicit "none of these", then ask a second, differently worded question ("does this method's output answer the question as asked") before accepting. Method descriptions would also be rewritten to say what each covers, how it differs from its nearest neighbour and what it excludes. The test is direct: on the enlarged held-out set, compare the current single choice against each step added in turn, and record how many of the nine known misroutes are caught and how many of the 19 correct routes are wrongly rejected. The evidence is suggestive, not strong. DAIR's digest of Skill++ reports routers selecting all required skills for 76.3% of queries with rewritten descriptions against 36.4% with the originals at 731 skills, and its digest of Demystifying Agent Skills reports correct-use precision falling from 29.6% to 3.3% as a pool grows from 5 to 100; both are DAIR summaries of coding-agent work ([DAIR 2026 list](https://github.com/dair-ai/ML-Papers-of-the-Week/blob/main/years/2026.md)). A paper on Jev, which appears from its name to be the same family as Agere's classifier, says reliability "depends on action-set size and on near-valid alternatives" ([REFLEX, abstract only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26532)). A tool-retrieval benchmark finds general-purpose retrievers perform poorly at picking tools without tool-specific training ([ToolRet, abstract only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01763)). Not supported: that a threshold change alone removes plausible-wrong picks; that the REFLEX classifier is the one Agere uses (inferred, unconfirmed); or any measured detection rate for wrong-but-plausible choices, which no paper reports.

**Rank 3, the promotion gate.** A promoted recipe is replayed without a model, so a wrong one repeats silently. The gate has two exposures. The warehouse copy may lack the rows that would separate two different queries (ties, blanks, empty groups, period edges), and two models reading the same schema text can share one misreading. On the first, a formal verifier that searches for a small database on which two queries differ cut the authors' measured accuracy of ten text-to-SQL methods by 9.8 to 13.5 points on a public benchmark, with 207 of one method's queries matching the reference on the test database yet separable on another; the paper states a slightly different range in its introduction, and found the reference query was often the faulty one, so disagreement signals two readings, not which is wrong ([SpotIt, full text of first half](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.26840v2)). A separate study required candidate queries to match on all ten differently seeded database copies ([unanimous-jury study, full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.18492v1)). On the second exposure, one paper names "consensus on hallucinations" and checks SQL candidates against an independent Python computation on a small purpose-built database, reporting up to 2.2 points over agreement voting ([DPC, abstract only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.15163)). The proposed change is to require row agreement on several small synthetic copies built to contain boundary rows, and to try a non-SQL second opinion on a sample. The test is to re-run the 180 promoted recipes through the harder gate and hand-check those that fail. Not supported, and the central gap of this report: **no paper measures the false-acceptance rate of two different models returning the same rows**. SpotIt measures model against reference; the two-model rate is an extrapolation. The jury study also shows the cost side: adding judges lowered false accepts and correct accepts together, so a harder gate will reject some good recipes, and only a labelled sample reveals how many.

## Five medium-value changes tune what already exists

**Rank 4, fewer questions at the last rung.** The 3 to 25 cents and 17 to 55 seconds are best attacked by keeping questions away from the agent. Four moves have evidence. Group logged questions that fell to the frontier rungs, rank groups by total spend, and write a recipe or method binding for the costliest group first; an industry paper reports that targeting clustered escalation failures gave "over twice" the cost improvement of untargeted work ([RouteNLP, abstract only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23577)). Show the frontier SQL writer the nearest saved verified recipes as examples; one benchmark study reports +4.34 points on held-out first attempts and finds verification quality mattered while richer memory formats did not ([Crystallization, abstract only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.07213)). Test whether better written definitions beat a better model: a 100-question retail study reports a 4 KB definitions file adding 17 to 23 points while three frontier models were indistinguishable ([semantic-layer study, abstract only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25149)). And cache the fixed front part of the agent's prompt, where reported savings are 41% to 80% of cost and 13% to 31% of time to first token, with a warning that caching changing tool results can slow things down ([prompt-caching study, snippet](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06007)). Each is tested by the last rung's share of traffic and spend before and after. Not supported: that starting the frontier writer in parallel makes a 55-second answer interactive. The one full-text study measured about 20% time saved in its lossless setting, at higher token cost, on games and dialogue, never on an analytics ladder ([Speculative Actions, full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04371v1)). No paper was found on sending duplicate requests to two providers or on stopping the agent early.

**Rank 5, fitted cut-offs.** One global 0.8 treats every decision type alike. The alternative fits, per decision type, a curve from the classifier's probability to how often it was right, then sets each cut-off as the cheapest that meets a stated error target. In the one careful head-to-head, this beat fixed and raw-score thresholds at equal accuracy and cut calibration error from 0.12 to 0.03; that study had 22,500 calibration examples, one workload and a single author ([UCCI, full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.18796v1)). A caching paper makes the same case per saved entry, arguing that right and wrong matches overlap too much for any single threshold ([vCache, full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.03771v5)). The test is on a later slice of the log: wrong-accept and escalation rates against 0.8, with thin decision types pooled. Not supported: any guarantee at Agere's data size. No paper calibrates this kind of classifier with fewer than about 1,000 labelled decisions per task; a learned "nothing cheap will work" detector caught 56.9% of impossible queries on familiar data and 2.8% on an unseen dataset ([Switchcraft, full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.07112)); and DAIR's digest of a Jev judging paper says the threshold did not transfer across fallback models ([DAIR 2026 list](https://github.com/dair-ai/ML-Papers-of-the-Week/blob/main/years/2026.md)).

**Rank 6, recipe upkeep.** Promotion is gated; life after promotion is not. The library-drift papers report that unmanaged growth pushed performance below a no-library baseline, that retiring entries only after at least 100 uses with a net-negative record was safe, and that retiring on 20 uses collapsed the library to two entries and scored below baseline ([Ratchet, full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.22148v1); [Library Drift, full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.19576v1)). A caching study that matches on an exact structured key (measure, grouping, filters, time window) reports zero false hits across 1,395 controlled queries, and coverage rising from 37% to 80% when coarser or narrower questions are answered from a saved one; it achieved that by refusing ambiguous wording, where turning plain language into the key was right only 44.4% of the time ([OLAP caching, full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.19811v1)). The change is a per-recipe log of uses and later contradictions, a wrong-reuse count reported beside the no-model answer share, and follow-up questions keyed on their resolved context. Not supported: that 100 is the right number for SQL recipes (one model, one code benchmark), any wrong-reuse measurement on real traffic past a few hundred entries, or any method for invalidating recipes when definitions change.

**Rank 7, the batch learner.** Generating questions from table and column names guarantees neither answerability nor novelty. Two drop-in changes need no model training: write an executable query first and the question from it, and generate from clusters of logged failures while penalising shapes already in the library ([SENTINEL, full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.12908); [SQL-Zero, abstract only](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/170457)). The test is new distinct shapes promoted per 100 generated questions, scored on the untouched set. Not supported: every validated gain in this area came from training model weights, which Agere does not do, and one clinical benchmark found 56% of automatically generated reference answers defective on physician review ([ClinicalBench, abstract only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11143)).

**Rank 8, document readers.** The mandatory quote proves text exists, not that it supports the claim. A Veterans Affairs study over 919,783 notes removed 14.59% of model-positive extractions with rule and grounding filters, and found the reader much weaker on notes with no corroborating code (strict F1 0.77) than on its calibration set (0.86 to 0.90) ([VA validation study, full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.06028v1)). Reading only the most relevant quarter of a document behind cheaper screening questions cut cost to 0.59 times a two-model cascade in one study, which also reports that the same idea without those screening questions cost more than the baseline ([Task Cascades, full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.05536v1)). The test is a hand-labelled set of negation, family-history and historical traps against the current rule, with accuracy reported separately for corroborated and uncorroborated documents. Not supported: any statistical guarantee on a count summed over hundreds of documents, and any cost-per-document figure from a clinical paper.

## What the research rules out, and where it is silent

**Trained routers need far more data than this stack has.** The largest comparison, of 21 routing methods on five benchmarks, found the top five within 0.04 to 0.52 points of each other, a simple nearest-neighbour look-up in the top two everywhere, and thirteen times more training data worth 0.47 points ([Routing Plateau, full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.07587)). A trained router built on 77,891 queries lost its edge over the look-up on an unseen dataset ([Switchcraft, full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.07112)), and another study found 1,000 examples per dataset insufficient and 4,000 adequate ([RouterXBench, full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11877)). Agere has hundreds of logged outcomes. The look-up at rank 9 is the only router worth testing, on a split by time and by question family, and DAIR's digest of one paper warns that such look-ups collapsed under paraphrase ([DAIR 2026 list](https://github.com/dair-ai/ML-Papers-of-the-Week/blob/main/years/2026.md)). Whether it works at 300 to 800 rows is untested anywhere.

**Fine-tuning, voting and self-rated confidence lack support.** The one volume anchor for training a small model is about 2,000 filtered traces from a larger teacher, not a system's own agreement-verified queries, with regressions on one task family ([agent distillation, full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.17612v2)); repeated self-training is reported to collapse without safeguards ([ReSAIL, abstract only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.39306)). Five-sample voting on one model moved accuracy by -0.07 points at about five times the tokens ([open-model study, full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.29733)). Learned SQL checkers score 0.58 to 0.64 on a scale where 0.5 is chance, enough to order an audit queue and not to accept answers ([TraceSQL, abstract only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17795)). A model's stated confidence tracked its decision to commit more than its correctness in one study ([Kumaran, abstract only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29490)), though two unopened snippets point the other way, so this is unsettled.

**DAIR's material is a checklist, not evidence for this stack.** The Prompt Engineering Guide's context-engineering pages cite no papers and report no benchmarks ([DAIR context engineering](https://www.promptingguide.ai/agents/context-engineering)); its only text-to-SQL item is one unevaluated example prompt ([DAIR MySQL example](https://www.promptingguide.ai/prompts/coding/mysql-query)); and a scripted pass over the 2026 and late-2025 weekly lists found no text-to-SQL paper at all ([DAIR 2026 list](https://github.com/dair-ai/ML-Papers-of-the-Week/blob/main/years/2026.md)). DAIR's public advice to prefer fixed workflows where predictability and cost matter fits the ladder as built ([DAIR workflows vs agents](https://www.promptingguide.ai/agents/ai-workflows-vs-ai-agents)). **Paid Academy content was not read**: Pro courses, labs and workshops, including those on context engineering and agent evaluation, sit behind a $29-a-month sign-in, and the Academy connector in this environment needs the product owner to authorise it (via `/mcp`) before it can be used ([DAIR Academy pricing](https://academy.dair.ai/pricing)). Only public titles and outlines were seen, and the two most relevant free items, Elements of AI Agents and the Jev-as-a-Judge lab, were not opened either ([DAIR Academy catalogue](https://academy.dair.ai/courses)).

**Further gaps.** No benchmark was found for population-health, claims or payer analytics questions; the hospital-records benchmarks cover patient-level questions on other schemas, so Agere's own sets remain the only valid scoreboard. Several papers behind ranks 2 to 4 were read at abstract level only, and their limitations are unknown until the full text is read.

## Conclusion

The published work says more about what to stop considering than what to add. The ladder's design choices (code-chosen methods, verified recipes, a different second model, mandatory quotes) match or lead what the literature recommends, and the papers offer nothing better than the ladder's own deterministic result checks. The remaining weaknesses are in what counts as verified. "The query ran" and "two models agreed" are both softer labels than they appear, and every threshold, promotion and learned shortcut inherits that softness. A modest hand-labelled sample, a few hundred answers checked by a person, is therefore the highest-leverage asset the stack lacks, because it converts ranks 2 through 9 from plausible to measurable.

Two findings should change sequencing. Because pre-attempt routing has plateaued across the field, effort belongs in cheap checks after an attempt, which a ladder already makes natural. And because agreement can hide a shared error, the next gain in trust comes from making the second opinion different in kind, a different database copy or a different method of computing, more than from adding a third model of the same kind.

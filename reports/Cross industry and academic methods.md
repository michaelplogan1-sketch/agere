# Borrow the actuaries' math, not the traders'

The ideas worth bringing into Agere come mostly from actuarial practice, health-policy econometrics and benchmark design. The finance and physics ideas that sound most exciting add little. The best candidates fix problems Agere's buyers already have, using machinery Agere already owns. Exact CMS settlement rules with a "how much of this bonus is luck" readout would sit on top of the existing Monte Carlo contract valuation. Stop-loss layer pricing would sit on the same simulated losses. A comparison-group guard would stop care-management programs from claiming savings that are really regression to the mean: an MIT-led randomised trial showed a 38-point pre-post "drop" that the control group matched exactly. A wrong-answer-penalised reliability score with agreement-gated abstention would cover the text-to-SQL rung. Credibility and shrinkage would steady small panels. Option pricing helps only as a way to describe contracts: no published health study prices an ACO contract with Black-Scholes, and every serious source uses simulation of real-world costs, which Agere already does. Quantum and quantum-inspired optimisation is toy-scale or vendor-reported. Under fair benchmarks it loses to classical solvers on constrained scheduling problems, which is what hospital rostering and OR problems are. Order-book models carry over to hospitals only as analogy, and the one measured health test of self-exciting (Hawkes) models lost to linear regression. Everything below is researched only. Nothing has been run on Agere data. Most sources compare against a simple baseline, but almost none compare against an LLM alone. Many key figures were seen only in search snippets and are marked as unverified.

**How to read the evidence marks.** The research notes record whether each source page was opened and read, or seen only as a search-engine snippet. This report keeps that distinction. Figures followed by **[snippet, unverified]** come from search summaries and should be checked before anyone quotes them. Figures with no mark come from pages that were opened, though often only the abstract or summary page, not the full paper. No figure in this report was reproduced on Agere data.

Two cheap housekeeping changes did not make the ranked list because they change measurement, not buyer behaviour. Forecast checks should score pinball loss and interval coverage, and should consider adaptive conformal updating. Both match how probabilistic emergency department forecasting is evaluated in the literature ([Cardiff ORCA 157566](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/157566); [HAL 04539380](https://hal-cnrs.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-04539380v1)) **[snippet, unverified]**. In health count data, CUSUM wins for large late shifts and EWMA for small early ones ([Korea University record](https://pure.korea.ac.kr/en/publications/a-comparison-of-cusum-ewma-and-temporal-scan-statistics-for-detec/)) **[snippet, unverified]**. That same pair is the natural baseline for the matched-filter detector already proposed for retirement at AUC 0.55. This is an inference: no source tested it.

## Contract math gains from exact CMS rules, not option formulas

The shared-savings payoff really is option-shaped. The MSSP BASIC track has a minimum savings rate that acts as a gate. Sharing then runs at 40% for one-sided levels and 50% for two-sided levels, multiplied by a quality score. Savings are capped at **10% of the updated benchmark**, and two-sided levels carry a **30% shared-loss rate** with revenue- and benchmark-based loss limits ([42 CFR 425.605](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/425.605)). ACO REACH stacks risk corridors on top. From performance year 2026, the first Global corridor narrows **from 25% to 10%** and the quality withhold rises **from 2% to 5%** ([CMS ACO REACH PY2026 update](https://www.cms.gov/priorities/innovation/aco-reach-model-performance-year-2026-model-update-quick-reference)). Crossing the minimum savings rate pays from the first dollar, which creates a step in the payoff. Two things follow. The expected settlement is not the settlement at expected spend. And any sensitivity Agere shows near the gate will spike.

Nothing in the literature replaces simulation with a pricing formula. The only published "option pricing" of shared savings, Friedberg and colleagues in AJMC, used **bootstrap simulation of 77,364 commercial members' costs, not Black-Scholes**. It found that with 50% sharing and no real savings, a 500-patient provider collects **$225 per patient (3.1% of cost) from chance alone**, against **$23 (0.3%)** at 60,000 patients. About 31% of small providers profit purely by luck. Truncating outliers at the 99th percentile cut that chance value by about half at every panel size, more than any other contract feature ([AJMC 2013](https://www.ajmc.com/view/option-pricing-a-flexible-tool-to-disseminate-shared-savings-contracts)). An actuarial study of Medicare data found that a typical ACO has a **5–8% chance of a settlement with the wrong sign**, rising to **26–28% for diabetes-heavy and about 23% for cancer-heavy populations** ([Duncan et al., NAAJ 2023](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/uaajxx/v27y2023i2p242-252.html)). A consulting analysis of performance years 2020–22 found two-sided ACOs **forfeited $365M of savings to the minimum savings rate while the loss-side rate protected them from $149M** ([Milliman 2024](https://www.milliman.com/en/insight/mssp-minimum-savings-loss-rates-msr-mlr)). That is a ready-made test target for an election comparator.

These findings support item 1 of the shortlist. Agere's existing Monte Carlo already samples the real-world cost distribution, which is what every health source uses. The gap is the payoff layer. The rules should be applied exactly as written, versioned by performance year and track, and each parameter should cite its regulation or specification version. Two readouts can be added from the same draws: contract value at zero true savings (Friedberg's "chance payout") and the probability that the settled sign differs from the true sign (Duncan's error rate). The acceptance test is concrete. Reproduce a public-use-file ACO's reported settlement from its benchmark, expenditure and quality score. An LLM alone can explain the contract but cannot produce these numbers, because they depend on the population's cost distribution. That is an inference: no source tested an LLM on it.

Stop-loss pricing (item 4) uses the same draws. An actuarial society newsletter gives typical ACO aggregate stop-loss terms: **attachment at 105% of expected claims, exposure capped at 10% of expected claims, and a rate on line of 1–2%**. Premium loads total about 30%, and the newsletter calls aggregate ACO stop-loss "a relatively new and volatile risk" with thin market capacity ([SOA Reinsurance News, Dec 2023](https://www.soa.org/sections/reinsurance/reinsurance-newsletter/2023/december/rsn-2023-12-troutment/)). For a CFO holding a quote, the expected layer loss on Agere's simulated distribution, plus loading, is the fair-price check. The CVaR reduction per premium dollar answers whether to buy. Because health costs are heavily skewed (the top 20% of patients drive about 80% of spend in Friedberg's data), the simulated layer should beat a normal approximation. That is an inference, and no published accuracy comparison of ACO stop-loss methods was found.

The rest of the derivatives toolkit does not transfer. Risk-neutral pricing needs a traded, replicable underlying asset, and an ACO's population spend is neither, so Black-Scholes would misprice the gated, capped payoff and look more precise than it is. Real options for staged capacity has one quantified hospital case: a flexible PET-centre design valued at **CHF 167.18M, 20% above the basic design** ([Strategic Engineering case](https://strategic-engineering.co/blog/case/evaluating-initial-building-designs-considering-possible-future-changes-the-example-of-the-new-pet-centre-of-the-university-hospital-of-zurich)) **[snippet, unverified; page returned 404]**. Even that case used simulation with decision rules, not lattices, and nothing was back-tested. Market hedges have a poor record. The World Bank's pandemic bonds paid about **$195.8M more than five weeks after the WHO declaration**, did not trigger for Ebola, and were not renewed ([Insurance Journal](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/international/2020/12/10/593490.htm)). For providers, the levers worth modelling are contractual: elections, corridors, stop-loss and carve-outs.

## Program evaluation fails buyers through regression to the mean, not missing estimators

The most persuasive buyer-facing result in the whole research set is a negative one. In the Camden "hotspotting" randomised trial (MIT Economics and J-PAL, NEJM 2020), 800 high-utilising patients were randomised to intensive care management or usual care. **180-day readmission was 62.3% versus 61.7%**, an adjusted difference of 0.82 points with a confidence interval of −5.97 to 7.61. A before-and-after look at the program group suggested a **decline of about 38 percentage points**, and the control group showed "the exact same pattern" ([MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2020/health-care-hotspotting-no-effect-0108)). Patients enrol when they are at their sickest and most expensive, and they drift back toward average either way. Most care-management, VBC and command-centre claims a hospital CFO hears are built the same way. Item 2 makes Agere show the pre-post number next to an eligible-but-not-enrolled comparison, every time. It is cheap and explainable. It beats the simple method buyers use now, and an RCT quantifies how wrong that method can be.

The more formal estimators cover situations Agere's causal forests and double ML do not. Those assume no unmeasured confounding at a single time point. Programs usually roll out in waves. A 2025 practitioner's guide shows that two-way fixed effects under staggered timing uses already-treated units as controls. Callaway-Sant'Anna avoids this by estimating effects cohort by cohort ([Baker et al., arXiv 2503.13323](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13323v3)) **[snippet, unverified]**. Synthetic difference-in-differences weights donor units and pre-periods so that the parallel-trends assumption nearly holds. That suits a single hospital's command centre or a single plan's policy change ([Arkhangelsky et al., AER 2021](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.20190159)) **[snippet, unverified]**. The TARGET reporting guideline (JAMA 2025) turns target trial emulation into a 21-item protocol that Agere could bind to its decision context ([Peer Review Congress abstract](https://peerreviewcongress.org/abstract/transparent-reporting-of-observational-studies-emulating-a-target-trial-the-target-guideline/)) **[snippet, unverified]**. The honest limitation: no head-to-head was found showing synthetic DiD or Callaway-Sant'Anna beating matched pre-post on a care-management program. Item 7 therefore ranks below the simpler guard.

The same discipline applies to the operational claims buyers bring. Johns Hopkins leaders attribute **about a 20% drop in ED boarding** to their capacity command centre, but every figure is self-reported pre-post, with no peer-reviewed evaluation found ([Advisory Board 2018](https://www.advisory.com/daily-briefing/2018/06/11/command-center)) **[snippet, unverified]**. The MIT-built discharge predictions at Hartford HealthCare reached out-of-sample AUCs of 75.7–92.5%, were used by more than 200 clinicians across seven hospitals, and were followed by a reported **0.67-day drop in length of stay**. The abstract does not describe a control design, and the $55–72M yearly benefit is a projection ([arXiv 2305.15629](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15629v1)). Agere's comparative advantage is to supply the credible counterfactual for such claims, not to add another predictor.

Prediction models face a parallel problem, which item 6 addresses. A widely used proprietary sepsis model scored an **AUC of 0.63 at Michigan Medicine, against the developer's reported 0.76–0.83**. It alerted on 18% of inpatients and missed 67% of sepsis cases ([Contagion Live](https://www.contagionlive.com/view/evaluation-of-sepsis-prediction-tool-raises-questions-about-proprietary-tools)) **[snippet, unverified]**. The two credible outcome studies tie a model to a defined human action. In Penn's stepped-wedge trial, a mortality model plus clinician nudges raised serious-illness conversations **from 3.4% to 13.5%** ([Penn Medicine](https://www.pennmedicine.org/news/machine-learning-improves-end-of-life-care-for-cancer-patients)) **[snippet, unverified]**. Kaiser Permanente's deterioration alert was associated with an **adjusted 30-day mortality relative risk of 0.84** across 19 hospitals ([Kaiser Permanente](https://about.kaiserpermanente.org/news/press-release-archive/real-time-alerts-associated-with-lower-mortality)) **[snippet, unverified]**. A multi-hospital study found calibration drift without any loss of net benefit, so drift monitors should track usefulness, not calibration alone ([PMC11681292](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11681292/)) **[snippet, unverified]**. In the Obermeyer study, an algorithm trained to predict cost understated the needs of Black patients: correcting the label would raise their auto-identification **from 17.7% to 46.5%** ([Scientific American](https://scientificamerican.com/article/racial-bias-found-in-a-major-health-care-risk-algorithm/)) **[snippet, unverified]**. That makes a label-choice audit a necessary check on any plan risk list. Stanford's FURM process adds a simulation of realised benefit under workflow capacity ([arXiv 2403.07911](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.07911v2.pdf)) **[snippet, unverified]**. Agere's Monte Carlo and queueing components can run that simulation with no new modelling family.

## Text-to-SQL and note reading: measure reliability before adding models

The EHRSQL 2024 shared task is the benchmark closest to Agere's frontier SQL rung, because it scores whether a system knows when not to answer. Its reliability score gives +1 for a correct answer or a correct abstention and −10 for any wrong answer. **Abstaining on everything scores 20.** The winner scored 81.32. The second-place team scored 74.89 by requiring three frontier models to agree before answering, and the fourth-place team abstained whenever sampled queries disagreed. Pipelines with a separate answerability classifier scored only **36–44**. No system scored above zero once every error counted fully ([EHRSQL 2024 overview](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.06673v2)). Conformal abstention calibrates an agreement threshold on held-out data to a provable bound on wrong answers ([arXiv 2405.01563](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.01563)). Item 3 combines the two. Agere would report RS(10) against "always abstain" and "saved recipes only", and gate answers on agreement between executed results, calibrated to a target error rate. This extends the earlier stack report's proposals to penalise wrong answers and fit cut-offs. The new element is a clinical benchmark showing that agreement gating beat a standalone classifier.

The case for governed definitions is plausible but thinly evidenced, which is why item 8 is a measurement. On an enterprise insurance schema, a frontier LLM answered **16% correctly over raw SQL and 54% over a knowledge graph** of the same data ([Sequeda et al., arXiv 2311.07509](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07509)). That 16% figure is effectively "LLM alone". A semantic-layer tool vendor reports a move from about 84–90% to 98–100% on 11 questions ([vendor blog](https://docs.getdbt.com/blog/semantic-layer-vs-text-to-sql-2026?version=2)) **[snippet, unverified; vendor, tiny sample]**. Every top BIRD score (79.8–83.4 against a human 92.96) uses per-question hint text, which amounts to a miniature semantic layer ([BIRD leaderboard](https://bird-bench.github.io/)). On cost, the CHESS schema selector **cut LLM tokens five-fold for about +2 points of accuracy** ([arXiv 2405.16755](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16755)). No independent healthcare replication exists, so Agere should run its own three-arm ablation, reporting RS(10) and cost per question.

The clarifying conversation already in Agere deserves a measured A/B test, not an assumption that it helps. On BIRD-Interact, the best models succeed on only **about 16–17%** of interactive tasks ([BIRD leaderboard](https://bird-bench.github.io/)). CRAFT-MD found diagnostic accuracy falls when models must gather facts through dialogue rather than read a vignette ([HMS news](https://hms.harvard.edu/news/how-good-are-ai-clinicians-medical-conversations)). The claimed jump from 42.5% to 92.5% with clarification comes from a 40-query demo and was not visible on the opened page ([AmbiSQL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.15276)) **[snippet, unverified]**. The comparator should be "ask nothing, state the assumption". In Stanford's MedHELM, frontier LLMs scored lowest on **Administration & Workflow (0.53–0.63)** against 0.73–0.85 on note generation. Its LLM jury agreed with clinicians at an ICC of 0.47, slightly above clinician-to-clinician agreement at 0.43 ([MedHELM arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23802)). That category is the one nearest Agere's questions. It argues for execution-based scoring wherever possible, and LLM judges only where checked against clinician labels.

For note reading, the evidence supports keeping a fine-tuned small encoder as the default extractor. Across 1,588 notes, an open 70B LLM beat fine-tuned BERT by only **about 1 F1 point on entities and 1.5–3.7 on relations** when training data was ample. It gained **7 points on an unseen institution**, but ran **up to 28× slower** ([Hu et al., arXiv 2411.10020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10020v4)). Medical coding is where an LLM alone clearly fails. A frontier LLM's best exact match (NEJM AI 2024) was **33.9% on ICD-10-CM** ([ScienceDaily summary of NEJM AI 2024](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/04/240422120603.htm)). The best reproducible open coder reaches **micro-F1 58.5 but macro-F1 21.1** on MIMIC-IV ICD-10 and never correctly predicts 51.8% of codes ([Edin et al., arXiv 2304.10909](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.10909)). An agentic system with code lookup reportedly reached 0.560 on rare codes against 0.227 for that open coder ([EMNLP 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1583.pdf)) **[snippet, unverified]**. Item 9 therefore targets a narrower, revenue-relevant question: which HCCs are documented in notes but absent from claims, and which coded HCCs lack supporting evidence? A model proposes evidence spans. A deterministic mapper such as hccpy (Apache-2.0, CMS-HCC V22–V28) ([PyPI hccpy](https://pypi.org/project/hccpy)) is the rules baseline it must beat on precision and cost per chart. Earlier small readers failed Agere's 28-item calibration check, so every reader faces the same check.

Small open SQL models (item 13) beat a frontier model on general benchmarks but trail it on clinical schemas. OmniSQL-7B scores 87.9 on Spider test against 83.2 for a frontier model, but **34.9 on EHRSQL against 44.9** ([arXiv 2503.02240](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.02240)). Arctic-Text2SQL-R1-7B scores 36.7 on EHRSQL ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/Snowflake/Arctic-Text2SQL-R1-7B)). Their value would be cost on governed, definition-bound questions, so they rank low until items 3 and 8 show where the frontier writer is over-spending.

On forecasting, Agere already holds the leaders. On fev-bench's 100 tasks, Chronos-2 improves on seasonal naive by **47.3%** on its probabilistic skill score and wins 91.4% of comparisons. TiRex follows at 42.6% and TimesFM-2.5 at 42.2%. Covariates lift Chronos-2 from 40.9% to 47.0% on the tasks that have them ([fev-bench, arXiv 2509.26468](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.26468)). Those are averages over energy, retail and web series. Agere's series (monthly admissions, PMPM, ED arrivals) are shorter and change regime more often, so the margins may shrink. The AutoETS rows were not captured, so the margin over ETS specifically is unconfirmed. A platelet study is a useful reminder: in the Aachen replication, LASSO matched an LSTM ([PMC8848235](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8848235/)) **[snippet, unverified]**. Foundation forecasters should have to beat a regularised linear model, not the other way round. TiRex and Toto add little beyond what Agere already holds and stay parked.

Tabular foundation models now lead parts of TabArena, but under limits. In the v0.1 paper, tuned CatBoost ranked first under conventional tuning. TabPFNv2 was only evaluated on datasets with at most 10,000 rows and 500 features ([TabArena, arXiv 2506.16791](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.16791v4)). A secondary snapshot puts foundation models about 240 Elo above the best GBDT ([Codesota snapshot](https://www.codesota.com/tasks/tabular-ml)) **[secondary source, unverified]**, and the strongest of those weights are non-commercial. More importantly, Agere's own admission, discharge and no-show predictors are archived because 4 of 5 tasks could not be fitted. A stronger learner does not fix missing task data, so item 14 is parked until there is a fittable task.

De-identification is a deployment prerequisite, not a buyer feature, so it sits outside the ranking. The realistic open stack is a transformer de-identifier (**F1 98.9 on i2b2 2014**) ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/StanfordAIMI/stanford-deidentifier-base)) layered with a rules system. Philter reports **99.92% recall** on i2b2 2014, though under its own Safe-Harbor preprocessing ([npj Digit Med 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7156708)) **[snippet, unverified]**. A residual-PHI audit sample sits on top.

## Operations methods with measured hospital transfer are few, small and retrospective

Only a handful of cross-industry methods have measured transfer to hospitals, and the best of them come from inventory and airline revenue management. Stanford's platelet model forecast demand three days ahead from 43 EMR features. In a 29-month retrospective analysis it **cut platelet expiration from 10.5% to 3.2%** while keeping at least 10 units on the shelf, and was later reported in use at the blood center ([PMC5664553](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5664553)) **[snippet, unverified]**. The roughly $80M national saving is a press projection, not a measurement. That makes item 11 the strongest operational transfer, held back only by data: blood-bank inventory is not in Agere's warehouse. The acceptance metric is simulated outdate percentage and stockout days against historical ordering on held-out months.

No-show overbooking (item 12) is an airline transfer with a modest but real published gain. Overbooking only the slots where a no-show is predicted, rather than at a flat rate, gave **at least 6% less patient waiting, 27% less overtime and 3% lower total cost** ([Huang & Hanauer, ACI 2014](https://www.thieme-connect.com/products/ejournals/abstract/10.4338/ACI-2014-04-RA-0026)) **[snippet, unverified]**. No-show models typically reach AUC 0.82–0.85, but at a practical threshold the positive predictive value can be as low as 0.18 ([BMC HSR 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12913-023-09969-5)) **[snippet, unverified]**. So schedule from calibrated probabilities, not thresholded flags. The right baseline is flat overbooking at the clinic's mean no-show rate, not zero overbooking. Agere's own no-show predictor could not be fitted, so this item waits on data.

Credibility and shrinkage (item 5) is the most reusable method in the set. The actuarial standard of practice on credibility recognises greatest-accuracy (Bühlmann) credibility, Z = n/(n+k). It also requires that comparison experience resemble the subject population, and says experience that cannot be adjusted to meet this "should not be used" ([ASOP 25 revision page](https://www.actuarialstandardsboard.org/asops/credibility-procedures-2/)). That requirement maps directly onto Agere's population and date-scope binding. Mathematically, it is the same empirical-Bayes shrinkage used in hospital profiling, where misclassification depends on reliability, sample-size spread and tier cut-offs ([He, Selck & Normand, Stat Med 2014](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/76303)). One implementation can serve the CFO (PMPM credibility) and quality (the posterior probability that a provider exceeds a threshold). That output is honest about uncertainty and can return a supported "unknown". The baseline is the raw rate with minimum-n suppression. The test is out-of-time prediction error and the stability of tier assignments.

Bornhuetter-Ferguson (item 10) has the weakest evidence on the shortlist and stays only because it is cheap. Practitioners agree it stabilises the most recent incurral months by leaning on an expected PMPM, and that chain-ladder is better for mature months. No health-specific head-to-head was found. A commissioned actuarial study of health IBNR accuracy exists, but its link returned 404. Machine-learning reserving "has not become a gold standard" ([Richman & Wüthrich, arXiv 2602.15385](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.15385v1)) **[snippet, unverified]**. The test is a rolling-origin backtest of months 0–3 comparing chain-ladder, BF and Benktander, plus interval coverage of Mack's method against Agere's conformal intervals.

## Skip / park and why

The negative findings matter as much as the shortlist, because each one is a plausible-sounding pitch that the evidence does not support.

| Idea | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Black-Scholes and risk-neutral closed forms for ACO contracts or capacity | **Skip** | No traded underlying, skewed non-lognormal costs, annual settlement. No health source uses them. Every source uses real-world simulation, which Agere already has ([AJMC](https://www.ajmc.com/view/option-pricing-a-flexible-tool-to-disseminate-shared-savings-contracts)). At most, a closed form can serve as a unit-test oracle for the payoff engine |
| Market hedges (cost-trend index futures, healthcare-inflation ETFs, pandemic bonds) | **Skip** | Large basis risk against any attributed population; pandemic bonds paid late and were not renewed ([CGD](https://www.cgdev.org/blog/good-idea-executed-badly-why-world-bank-should-not-renew-pandemic-emergency-facility-insurance)) |
| Real options for staged capacity builds | **Park** | Real CFO question, but needs capex and margin data Agere does not hold; evidence is unvalidated case studies |
| Quantum annealing, hybrid quantum solvers, QAOA for rostering or OR scheduling | **Skip** | The largest hospital study rostered **3–4 nurses**, often failed to find a feasible roster, and had no classical baseline ([arXiv 1904.12139](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1904.12139)). In the fairest benchmark, a hybrid annealing service was **about 4.6× worse than a commercial MILP solver** on a full-scale constrained scheduling problem ([Quinton et al., arXiv 2409.05542](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.05542v4)). Its one win was unconstrained binary quadratic problems, which hospital scheduling is not. The field's consensus review claims no demonstrated advantage ([Abbas et al., arXiv 2312.02279](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.02279)) |
| Quantum machine learning for risk prediction | **Skip** | A systematic review found only 16 of 4,915 records tested under realistic conditions, with no consistent advantage over classical models ([arXiv 2410.02446](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.02446v2)) **[snippet, unverified]**. Consistent with Agere's retired tensor-train classifier |
| Quantum-inspired digital annealers for OR block allocation | **Park** | One vendor-reported **37% gain in prime-time minutes**, with no classical baseline and no independent evaluation ([HospiMedica](https://www.hospimedica.com/latest-news/articles/294798729/surgical-capacity-optimization-solution-helps-hospitals-boost-or-utilization.html)) **[snippet, unverified]**. If a buyer names the OR-block problem, solve it with open CP-SAT or MILP against a release-unused-blocks rule |
| Fault-tolerant quantum computing | **Park** | Vendor and DARPA targets of 2029–2033; early uses expected in chemistry, not scheduling. Re-review only after an independently verified scheduling win over classical solvers |
| Queue-reactive and birth-death order-book models | **Skip** | No healthcare use found; the transferable part is ordinary state-dependent queueing, which Erlang and simulation already cover |
| Hawkes self-exciting processes for arrivals or infection clusters | **Park** | The one measured health test found pure Hawkes visit-timing error of **3.241 against 2.003 for linear regression** on heart-failure data, and it lost on every dataset ([MEDCAS, ar5iv 2102.02586](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2102.02586)). Possible later use: a narrow cluster test for infection onsets, compared against CUSUM and SaTScan |
| Market-making inventory models, order-flow imbalance | **Skip** | Analogy only. The defensible core is newsvendor buffer sizing for float pools, which is itself parked: no controlled study was found, and only vendor claims exist |
| Kidney exchange, residency match | **Skip** | Real market-design successes, but run by national clearinghouses; not a buyer question Agere answers |
| OR time auctions, nurse shift bidding, dynamic shift pricing | **Park** | Academic designs and conflicting vendor numbers; at most a descriptive fill-rate and premium-pay SQL recipe |
| Revenue-management protection levels for elective OR capacity | **Park** | Modelling only. Protecting capacity by reimbursement class raises equity problems; urgency class is the defensible framing |
| Autonomous ICD coding from full notes (open coders, plain LLM coding) | **Skip** | Open state of the art has macro-F1 21.1 and MIMIC-bound weights; frontier-LLM exact match was 33.9% on ICD-10-CM. Only retrieve-and-verify HCC gap finding is trialled (item 9) |
| New sepsis or deterioration predictor | **Skip** | Needs real-time clinical integration and clinical qualification outside Agere's scope |
| Command-centre capacity board; ML plus robust-optimisation bed assignment | **Park / skip** | Evidence is self-reported pre-post or paper-stage. Agere's value is evaluating such claims (items 2 and 7) |
| HealthBench, leaderboard SQL agents, clinical summarisation, MedGemma | **Park** | Consumer-chat focus, non-healthcare tuning, individual-case scope, or unclear licence terms respectively |
| Non-commercial weights (TabPFN-2.5+, LimiX-2, Moirai-2, default TabPFN-TS, MIMIC-trained checkpoints) | **Skip for product** | Licence blocks commercial use |
| Aviation checklists, Lean, home-health routing | **Skip / park** | Checklists show strong transfer (surgical mortality 1.5% to 0.8%, before-after design) ([Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2009/01/surgical-safety-checklist-drops-deaths-and-complications-by-more-than-one-third/)) **[snippet, unverified]**, but they are care-process changes, not analytics; routing evidence is vendor-only |

## What this research did not cover

Several gaps limit how far these conclusions reach. Sports analytics (WAR-style value, Elo ratings) and astronomy methods (survey anomaly detection) were not researched at all and should not be scored from these notes. Many primary pages were blocked by bot checks, returned 403 or 404, or were PDFs that were not downloaded. Affected sources include PMC, eCFR, INFORMS, Thieme, the JAMA papers behind the sepsis, Penn and Kaiser figures, the CMS settlement guides that hold the exact REACH corridor bands and Level E loss limits, the actuarial society's health IBNR accuracy study, and the live TabArena and GIFT-Eval leaderboards. Many figures in this report therefore rest on news summaries or search snippets and are marked accordingly. Nothing was tested on Agere data: every accuracy, cost and licence statement is as published or as read from a model card. Most sources compare against a simple baseline, but almost none compare against an LLM alone. The exceptions are EHRSQL, the LLM-coding study, the knowledge-graph SQL study and MedHELM, so the "beats an LLM alone" half of the entry rule is untested for most items. Other specific gaps: no healthcare replication of semantic-layer gains, no conformal text-to-SQL study on clinical or claims data, no health head-to-head of BF against chain-ladder, no peer-reviewed controlled test of predictive overbooking, no prospective outcome from the platelet deployment, no AutoETS margin captured for the foundation forecasters, and unverified licences for TabICL, Philter, hccinfhir and LimiX-2M.

## Conclusion

The research changes where to look for leverage. Exotic mathematics is the wrong place to search, because the borrowed methods that hold up are the unglamorous ones that financial and insurance institutions already use: settlement rules applied exactly, layer pricing, credibility, and honest comparison groups. Each one turns a number Agere already computes into an answer a CFO can act on and defend. The same pattern holds on the language side. The most useful outside contributions are scoring rules and abstention gates, not new models: a benchmark that charges ten points per wrong answer, and an agreement check that knows when to stay silent. That inverts the usual product temptation. The next gains come from making existing components exact, versioned and measurable against "always abstain", "plain pre-post" and "the LLM on its own".

The second implication is about sequencing. The items that need new data feeds (platelets, no-shows, tabular foundation models) have the most striking published effects, but they face the same wall that archived Agere's own predictors: tasks that cannot be fitted. Items 1 through 8 need no new data family. They also build the measuring tools (RS(10), comparison groups, decision curves, credibility intervals) that every later candidate, including any future quantum or market-design pitch, would have to pass. Building those first lets later triage run on Agere's own numbers rather than on press releases.
